# Interests and personality

## Baseball

Suyu grew up watching Taiwanese baseball — international tournaments
especially — and MLB, mostly following Asian players. His childhood
favourites were Chien-Ming Wang with the Yankees, Hong-Chih Kuo with the
Dodgers, Hideki Matsui, and Ichiro Suzuki with the Mariners.

He started following the Blue Jays seriously at the end of the 2024 season,
so roughly two seasons. It began with a student event in Toronto in 2024
that took him to his first game at Rogers Centre, against the Cardinals.
The Blue Jays were having a poor season, but they won that night, and the
atmosphere stayed with him. He thought the ballpark itself was beautiful.

He went to a number of games in 2025. The season caught fire for him around
Canada Day — he was at the July 2 game against the Yankees, part of a series
the Blue Jays swept, and both the play and the crowd were at a high.

What struck him most was how the crowd treats its own players: loud,
unreserved encouragement, standing ovations, applause given freely. He
contrasts it with Taiwanese crowds, where cheering tends to be led by
someone up front. He prefers the Toronto version — it feels less
choreographed.

**Games he brings up:**

- **2025 ALDS Game 2 vs the Yankees.** His first postseason game in person.
  Rookie Trey Yesavage struck out 11, and Vladimir Guerrero Jr. hit a grand
  slam that, in Suyu's words, blew the place open. His first taste of a
  top-tier home playoff win, and the one he calls unforgettable.
- **2025 ALCS Game 2 vs the Mariners.** He was there for this one too, and
  the Blue Jays were blown out. The stadium went quiet — "a library" — and
  watching the crowd turn their caps inside out was a piece of baseball
  culture he had not seen before.
- **2025 ALCS Game 7 vs the Mariners.** He watched at home. George
  Springer's go-ahead three-run home run got him out of his chair, and
  downtown Toronto was car horns and shouting for the rest of the night.

**Players he likes:** his Blue Jays jersey is a George Springer. He rates
Daulton Varsho's centre-field defence very highly, and likes both Ernie
Clement's infield defence and his bat. He is a particular fan of catcher
Alejandro Kirk — fans of other teams make jokes about Kirk's build and
speed, but Suyu points to his framing and his hitting, and takes him as
evidence that baseball asks less of a specific body type than most
professional sports. He finds that comparatively fair.

## Other sports

## BaZi (八字)

BaZi is Chinese birth-chart astrology. Suyu knew of it for a long time —
his mother consulted fortune tellers when he was young — but he only began
engaging with it himself in roughly the past year.

**His actual position: he believes in it.** But the route he took there
matters, and he has written about it publicly. He grew up keeping his
distance, because it read to him as rigid determinism — a fate already
written, leaving no room for agency, and he has never liked spoilers for
his own life.

That changed over a winter in Toronto. He watched his roommate and her
father work through BaZi charts on a video call with more nuance than he
expected: not about luck, but about understanding internal cycles in order
to make better decisions. He ran a quick experiment, feeding
centuries-old BaZi texts into an AI model to see whether it could parse the
logic of Stems and Branches. He found the output unsettlingly coherent. The
line from his roommate that shifted his view was: *"If you understand your
chart, you can change your habits and break your life's upper limit."* What
convinced him was the framing of BaZi as a tool for self-understanding and
decision-making, not as a prediction engine.

**How this squares with the pre-registered study.** Suyu's pre-registered
study asked a deliberately narrow question: can coarse, three-pillar BaZi
features predict MLB hitting outcomes? All 47 confirmatory hypotheses came
back null, and he reported them exactly as they landed rather than digging
for a positive result. He treats that as a genuine null on that specific
question, not as a verdict on BaZi as a whole — the charts used drop the
birth hour a full reading relies on, the v1 features are coarse, and MLB is
an extremely noisy setting for any birth-date signal. He states those as
limitations, not excuses. The thing he values in BaZi — a framework for
reflecting on his own cycles and habits — is not what that study measured,
and he does not claim the study supports it.

If asked whether the null result changed his mind: it settled the narrow
empirical question he posed, and he is comfortable holding both. The
discipline he is proud of is having built a study that *could* report
nothing, and then reporting it.

## Working with him

He is reliable about commitments and works hard to hit agreed delivery
dates; in his previous roles in Taiwan he had a strong track record on this
and was trusted accordingly by management. Colleagues he worked with were
comfortable relying on him.

When requirements came in from other departments, his habit was to organise
and design first, then send a written plan or specification back to the team
and the requester for confirmation before building. He is detail-oriented in
that step. (This is the same design-then-implement discipline described in
his notes on how he works, applied in a workplace setting.)

## Outside work

In Taiwan he went to the gym after work, four or five sessions a week. He
took it far enough to compete: he was a representative powerlifting athlete
at the Yilan County Citizens Sports Games in 2022, with a 205 kg squat,
120 kg bench press, and 230 kg deadlift.

He cooks for himself — his reasoning is that eating out constantly makes for
unbalanced nutrition and, he believes, more inflammation, so he treats diet
as one part of a set alongside training and rest. On weekends he likes
getting outdoors.
